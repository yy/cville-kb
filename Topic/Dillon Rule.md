The Dillon Rule is the doctrine that a local government holds only the powers the state has given it: those granted in express words, those necessarily implied by the express grants, and those essential to its declared purposes. Where there is reasonable doubt whether a power was conferred, it was not. Virginia is a Dillon Rule state and has not granted its localities home rule, so Charlottesville can act only where the General Assembly has delegated the authority. The name comes from John Forrest Dillon, an Iowa judge who stated the principle in an 1868 case and developed it in his 1872 treatise *Municipal Corporations*.

Virginia courts have applied the doctrine since 1882 and read it narrowly. *Commonwealth v. County of Arlington* (1977) is cited as the key case establishing the strength of Virginia's adherence. Efforts to loosen it, including recommendations from a Governor's Advisory Committee in 1992, have not succeeded. Fairfax County's official description of its own position states that Virginia local governments "have limited powers," including "in areas such as raising revenue," and cannot act in certain areas "without appropriate action from the state."

## Why it shows up locally

Nearly every city power traces back to a specific enabling statute in the Code of Virginia rather than to any general authority of the city itself.

- **Zoning.** Va. Code [§ 15.2-2280](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title15.2/chapter22/section15.2-2280/) lets "any locality" divide its territory into districts and "regulate, restrict, permit, prohibit, and determine" land use, structure size and height, open space, and mineral extraction. The [[Policy/Charlottesville Development Code]] exists inside that grant.
- **Proffers.** [[Process/Proffer]] are creatures of Va. Code § 15.2-2303, not of local invention.
- **Revenue.** Local taxing power is delegated item by item. Cvillepedia's example is Albemarle County's food and beverage tax, capped at 4% and requiring a voter referendum under the state's delegation.
- **Housing policy.** HousingForward Virginia notes that only seven named jurisdictions hold broad authority to require affordable set-asides, and that rent control, expanded tenant protections, and vacancy taxes are not available to Virginia localities at all.

The practical effect is that when the city cannot do something, the reason is often that Richmond has not authorized it, and the remedy is a bill in the General Assembly rather than an ordinance.
